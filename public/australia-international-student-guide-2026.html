2026 International Student Guide to Australia: Visa, Cost, and Application Essentials

Navigate your Australian study journey with our 2026 guide. Covering student visa updates, tuition fees, living costs, and application steps for international students.

Australia remains one of the most sought-after education destinations globally, with over 720,000 international student enrolments recorded by the Department of Education in early 2026. That number underlines both the country’s academic reputation and the fierce competition for places at its top institutions. For anyone planning to study in Australia, understanding the latest visa conditions, cost of living adjustments, and application timelines is no longer optional—it is essential.

This guide walks through the practical steps of preparing for an Australian education in 2026. It covers the Student visa (subclass 500) framework, updated financial evidence thresholds, tuition ranges across university groups, accommodation realities in major cities, and the academic documentation that admissions teams actually look for. The aim is to give you a clear, evidence-based roadmap without the marketing fluff.

Understanding the 2026 Student Visa (Subclass 500) Requirements

The Student visa (subclass 500) remains the primary pathway for international students enrolled in a full-time CRICOS-registered course. In 2026, the Department of Home Affairs continues to refine the Genuine Student (GS) framework, which replaced the older Genuine Temporary Entrant (GTE) requirement. Applicants now answer targeted questions about their study motivation, course relevance to career goals, and ties to their home country—directly inside the online visa form rather than submitting a separate statement.

Key Eligibility Criteria in 2026

  • Confirmation of Enrolment (CoE): You must hold a valid CoE for a registered course before lodging the visa. Conditional offers or letters of offer are not sufficient.
  • English Language Proficiency: Minimum scores remain broadly consistent with 2025 levels. Most undergraduate programs require IELTS 6.0–6.5 (or equivalent PTE Academic, TOEFL iBT), while postgraduate courses often demand IELTS 6.5–7.0. Always check individual university requirements, as some Group of Eight (Go8) programs set higher thresholds.
  • Financial Capacity: From 1 October 2025, the 12-month living cost evidence increased to AUD 24,505 for the primary applicant, with additional amounts for partners and dependants. This figure reflects actual inflation in housing and daily expenses.
  • Overseas Student Health Cover (OSHC): You must maintain OSHC for the entire visa duration. Single cover typically ranges from AUD 500 to AUD 700 per year, while family cover costs more.

The Department processed over 590,000 student visa applications in the 2024–25 program year, with a median processing time of 28 days for the higher education sector in early 2026. Applying at least 8–12 weeks before your course start date remains the safest strategy.

The Genuine Student Test in Practice

The GS assessment is not a tick-box exercise. Case officers evaluate whether your chosen course makes logical sense given your previous education and employment background. A mechanical engineer applying for a diploma in business management, for example, will face more scrutiny than someone pursuing a Master of Engineering. Write your GS responses in clear, specific language—mention course modules, research supervisors if applicable, and how the qualification will improve your employability back home.

Tuition Fees and Living Costs: What to Budget in 2026

Tuition fees in Australia vary significantly by institution type, course level, and discipline. The Australian Government’s Study Australia portal publishes annual benchmarks, but real costs often exceed those averages once you factor in laboratory fees, student services charges, and materials.

Group of Eight universities—such as the University of Melbourne, University of Sydney, and UNSW—generally sit at the upper end of these ranges. Regional universities and TAFE institutions often charge 15–25% less, and some offer scholarships that reduce the first-year fee significantly.

Scholarships are worth researching early. The Australia Awards program remains the flagship government-funded option for students from eligible developing countries. University-specific scholarships, like the University of Queensland International Excellence Scholarship or Monash International Merit Scholarship, can cut tuition by 20–50%. Most scholarship deadlines fall between August and November for Semester 1 intake the following year.

Updated Living Costs

The Department of Home Affairs’ AUD 24,505 figure is a visa requirement, not a comfortable living budget. In practice, students in Sydney and Melbourne report monthly expenses closer to AUD 2,200–2,800 when including rent, groceries, transport, and utilities.

A realistic breakdown for a single student in Melbourne (2026 averages):

  • Shared accommodation (inner suburbs): AUD 1,100–1,500/month
  • Groceries: AUD 400–550/month
  • Public transport: AUD 150–200/month (concession rates available in some states)
  • Phone and internet: AUD 50–80/month
  • Entertainment and incidentals: AUD 250–400/month

Brisbane, Adelaide, and Perth offer 10–20% lower housing costs, making them attractive for budget-conscious students. Hobart and Darwin remain the most affordable state capitals, though course options are more limited.

The Application Process: From Research to Enrolment

Applying to Australian universities involves several distinct stages. Rushing any one of them leads to delays or rejections.

Step 1: Course and Institution Research

Use the Commonwealth Register of Institutions and Courses for Overseas Students (CRICOS) to verify that your intended course is registered. Without CRICOS registration, you cannot obtain a student visa. The Quality Indicators for Learning and Teaching (QILT) website provides independent data on student satisfaction, graduate employment rates, and median salaries by institution and study area—raw numbers that matter more than marketing brochures.

Step 2: Preparing Your Documents

Admissions teams expect:

  • Certified academic transcripts (translated into English if necessary)
  • English test scores (valid within two years of your course start date)
  • Statement of purpose (institution-specific, not the same as the visa GS statement)
  • Referee letters (mainly for research degrees)
  • Portfolio (for creative disciplines)

Many universities now use digital verification platforms like Digitary or My eQuals. If your previous institution issues documents through these systems, sharing them speeds up assessment.

Step 3: Submitting Applications

Most undergraduate applications from international students go through state-based Tertiary Admissions Centres (TACs)—UAC in NSW/ACT, VTAC in Victoria, QTAC in Queensland. Postgraduate applications are usually direct to the university via their online portal. Application fees range from AUD 50 to AUD 150, though many universities waive these during promotional periods or for applicants from certain countries.

Step 4: Accepting the Offer and Paying Deposit

Once you receive an unconditional offer, you typically need to pay a first-semester tuition deposit (often AUD 9,000–15,000) to secure your place and receive the CoE. This deposit is separate from the OSHC payment and any accommodation booking fees.

Accommodation Options and What They Really Cost

Housing is the single largest expense for most international students. The 2026 rental market remains tight in Sydney and Melbourne, with vacancy rates hovering around 1.5–2.5% according to CoreLogic data. Starting your search early and understanding the trade-offs is critical.

On-Campus Residential Colleges and Halls

University-managed accommodation offers convenience and community but comes at a premium. A single room in a residential college at the Australian National University or University of Sydney can cost AUD 400–600 per week, often including meals. Self-catered halls are cheaper—around AUD 250–380 per week—but spots fill by November for the following February intake.

Private Rentals and Share Houses

Most students eventually move into private rentals. The median weekly rent for a unit in Sydney reached AUD 680 in early 2026 (Domain data), while Melbourne units averaged AUD 520. Sharing a house or apartment brings individual costs down to AUD 220–350 per week. Factor in bond (usually 4 weeks’ rent) and utility connections when calculating upfront costs.

Purpose-Built Student Accommodation (PBSA)

Providers like Scape, UniLodge, and Iglu operate buildings near major campuses. Weekly rates in 2026 range from AUD 350 to AUD 550 for a studio, with bills and internet usually included. PBSA is convenient but often more expensive than a comparable private share house. Read the contract carefully—some lock you into a full-year lease even if your course runs only 40 weeks.

Working While Studying: Rights and Realistic Expectations

Student visa holders can work up to 48 hours per fortnight during study terms and unlimited hours during scheduled breaks. This cap, reintroduced in mid-2023 after a temporary relaxation during the pandemic, is strictly enforced through payroll data matching.

Finding Part-Time Work

Hospitality, retail, and aged care remain the largest employers of international students. The national minimum wage sits at AUD 23.23 per hour (as of July 2025), with casual loading adding 25%. A student working 20 hours per week at minimum wage earns roughly AUD 460 per week before tax—enough to cover rent and groceries in most cities but little else.

Tax file number (TFN) registration is essential before starting any paid work. You can apply online through the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) once you arrive. Without a TFN, employers withhold tax at the top marginal rate.

Post-Study Work Rights and Pathways

The Temporary Graduate visa (subclass 485) allows eligible graduates to live and work in Australia after completing their studies. In 2026, the Post-Study Work stream offers:

  • Bachelor degree graduates: up to 2 years
  • Masters by coursework graduates: up to 3 years
  • Masters by research graduates: up to 3 years
  • Doctoral graduates: up to 4 years

Additional 1–2 years are available for graduates who studied and lived in designated regional areas. The government’s Migration Strategy, released in late 2025, signalled no major changes to these durations for 2026, but the points test for permanent skilled migration continues to favour applicants with Australian qualifications, work experience, and strong English skills.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start my application for the February 2027 intake? Begin researching courses by April–May 2026, sit English tests by June–July, submit applications by August–September, and lodge your visa by November–December. This timeline accounts for processing delays and scholarship deadlines.

Can I bring my family on a student visa? Yes, but the financial evidence requirement increases significantly. You must show an additional AUD 8,574 for a partner and AUD 3,670 per child in 2026, plus school fees for school-age dependants. The partner’s work rights depend on your course level—partners of Masters and PhD students have unrestricted work rights, while partners of Bachelor students face the 48-hour fortnightly cap.

Is it possible to change courses or institutions after arriving? Yes, but course changes that lower the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) level—such as moving from a Bachelor to a Diploma—require a new visa application. Always check with your institution’s international student advisor before making changes.

Do I need a police check for the visa? The Department may request a police certificate from any country where you have lived for 12 months or more in the past 10 years. You do not need to provide it upfront, but having one ready can prevent processing delays.
