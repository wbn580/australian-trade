Australian Trust Joint Ventures with China: Structures, Tax & Compliance

Explore how discretionary trust investment and unit trust cross-border structures shape Australia-China joint ventures. Learn key tax, legal, and operational considerations for 2026.

China remains Australia’s largest two-way trading partner, with goods and services trade reaching approximately AUD 320 billion in 2024–2025, according to the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade. For Australian enterprises and Chinese investors seeking to formalise cross-border collaboration, the choice of legal structure is pivotal. An Australian trust joint venture often emerges as a flexible, tax-efficient vehicle that can accommodate both passive investment and active operational roles. This article examines how discretionary trusts and unit trusts function within a China Australia business structure, the regulatory nuances that arise in 2026, and practical strategies for structuring a compliant, durable partnership.

Why Trust Structures Appeal to Cross-Border Joint Ventures

A trust separates legal ownership (vested in the trustee) from beneficial ownership (held by beneficiaries). This separation is especially valuable in a cross-border context, where Chinese investors may want exposure to Australian assets or business income without triggering unintended regulatory or tax consequences. In a unit trust cross-border model, investors subscribe for units much like shares in a company, gaining proportional entitlements to income and capital. By contrast, a discretionary trust investment framework gives the trustee power to distribute income among a class of beneficiaries, which can include Chinese corporate entities, family members of a founder, or charitable objects.

Key drivers behind the rise of trust-based joint ventures include:

  • Asset protection: Trust assets are generally shielded from creditors of individual beneficiaries, a feature valued by Chinese state-owned enterprises and high-net-worth families.
  • Tax flexibility: Trusts can stream income to beneficiaries in lower tax brackets, including foreign residents subject to withholding tax concessions under the Australia-China Double Tax Agreement (DTA).
  • Regulatory familiarity: While the trust concept does not exist in Chinese domestic law, it is increasingly recognised through offshore structures in Hong Kong and Singapore, making it easier for sophisticated Chinese counterparties to engage with an Australian trust.

The Australian Taxation Office (ATO) continues to scrutinise cross-border trust arrangements, particularly where profits are shifted to low-tax jurisdictions. In 2026, compliance with the Diverted Profits Tax and thin capitalisation rules remains a core concern for any China Australia business structure involving trust entities.

Discretionary Trusts in the Australia-China Context

A discretionary trust, often called a family trust, gives the trustee wide latitude to decide which beneficiaries receive distributions and in what proportions. When used as an Australian trust joint venture vehicle, the trust deed typically defines a broad class of beneficiaries that may include Chinese parent companies, Australian operating subsidiaries, and key individuals.

Control and Decision-Making

Chinese investors frequently seek influence over major decisions without triggering Australian residency or permanent establishment issues. The trust deed can appoint a Chinese entity as a co-trustee or appointor, granting the power to remove and replace the trustee. However, care must be taken: if the Chinese party exercises de facto control from offshore, the ATO may deem the trust to be a foreign trust, altering tax outcomes.

Tax Implications for Discretionary Distributions

Under Australia’s 2026 tax rules, a discretionary trust that distributes net income to a foreign resident beneficiary is subject to non-resident withholding tax on unfranked dividends, interest, and royalties. The rate is generally 30%, but the DTA with China often reduces this to 10% or 15%, depending on the nature of the distribution and the recipient’s ownership percentage. For a discretionary trust investment structure, streaming franked dividends to an Australian resident beneficiary and interest income to a Chinese lender can optimise after-tax returns, provided the trust deed and resolutions meticulously document the source of each distribution.

Land Tax and Stamp Duty Considerations

Several Australian states levy surcharge land tax and stamp duty on trusts that have foreign beneficiaries. In New South Wales and Victoria, a discretionary trust with any foreign beneficiary may attract surcharge purchaser duty of up to 8% and an annual land tax surcharge of 2–4%. Chinese investors contemplating real estate joint ventures must model these imposts early. Restructuring the trust as a unit trust cross-border entity, where foreign unitholders hold less than a prescribed threshold, can sometimes mitigate surcharges, though state revenue offices increasingly apply anti-avoidance provisions.

Unit Trusts as a Cross-Border Collaboration Tool

A unit trust issues fixed entitlements to income and capital, making it more analogous to a company. For Chinese institutional investors—such as private equity funds, state-owned enterprises, or listed corporates—a unit trust often provides the clarity and governance framework they require. The unit trust cross-border structure aligns well with joint ventures where profit shares, voting rights, and exit mechanisms must be precisely defined.

Structuring the Unit Holders

A typical China Australia business structure using a unit trust might involve:

  • An Australian proprietary company as trustee, with independent Australian resident directors to satisfy central management and control tests.
  • Chinese investors holding Class A units with preferential dividend rights but limited voting power, while Australian founders hold Class B units with enhanced governance rights.
  • A Hong Kong special purpose vehicle interposed for Chinese investors to access the Hong Kong–Australia DTA, which can offer more favourable capital gains treatment than the mainland China DTA.

The ATO’s Taxation Ruling TR 2023/4 (updated in 2025) clarifies when a unit trust is treated as a fixed trust for tax purposes. If the trustee has any discretion to vary unit holder entitlements, the trust may be classified as a hybrid, losing the flow-through benefits of a fixed trust. Drafting the trust deed with rigid distribution clauses is therefore essential for cross-border certainty.

Withholding Tax and Capital Gains

For Chinese unitholders, the key tax events are distributions and exit. Managed Investment Trust (MIT) withholding rates apply if the unit trust qualifies as an MIT, reducing the rate on fund payments to 15% for residents of exchange-of-information countries (which includes China). However, most joint venture unit trusts do not meet the widely held requirements for MIT status unless they attract substantial third-party capital. Non-MIT unit trusts face the default 30% withholding, subject to DTA relief.

On exit, Chinese investors selling units in an Australian trust that holds taxable Australian property (TAP)—including mining rights, commercial real estate, and interests in land-rich entities—must consider Australian capital gains tax. The 2026 Federal Budget confirmed that the TAP rules will continue to capture indirect disposals, so Chinese vendors should obtain a clearance certificate or anticipate a 12.5% withholding at settlement.

Regulatory Compliance and FIRB Approval

The Foreign Investment Review Board (FIRB) regime applies to Chinese investors acquiring interests in Australian trusts. A discretionary trust investment by a foreign person typically requires FIRB approval because the foreign beneficiary’s interest is not fixed; the trustee’s discretion means the foreign person is treated as holding the maximum possible entitlement. For a unit trust, the FIRB threshold for Chinese private investors remains at AUD 0 for sensitive sectors (media, telecommunications, defence) and AUD 15 million for non-sensitive agribusiness, rising to AUD 1.4 billion for non-sensitive sectors under the Australia–China Free Trade Agreement, though national security screening introduced in 2021 applies universally.

In 2026, FIRB conditionally exempts certain passive unit trust cross-border investments from mandatory notification if the trust is widely held and the investor acquires less than 10%. However, joint venture trusts rarely meet the widely held test, so most Chinese investors should budget for FIRB application fees (ranging from AUD 4,000 to over AUD 1 million depending on transaction value) and a 40–60 day review period.

Anti-money laundering (AML) compliance is another layer. AUSTRAC requires trustees to identify beneficial owners behind Chinese corporate unitholders, which can be complex when state-owned entities are involved. Trust deeds should include know-your-customer (KYC) covenants obliging unitholders to disclose ultimate control, with redemption or forced transfer clauses for non-compliance.

Drafting the Trust Deed: Critical Clauses for Cross-Border JVs

A well-drafted trust deed is the backbone of any Australian trust joint venture. Beyond standard provisions, cross-border deeds should address:

  • Governing law and dispute resolution: Specify Australian governing law, but consider arbitration under the Singapore International Arbitration Centre (SIAC) or Hong Kong International Arbitration Centre (HKIAC) rules, as Chinese parties often prefer neutral venues and enforceable awards under the New York Convention.
  • Currency and repatriation: Define whether distributions are declared in AUD or RMB, and address the trustee’s obligation to facilitate foreign exchange approvals through China’s State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE).
  • Deadlock mechanisms: In 50/50 unit trusts, include put/call options, Texas shoot-out clauses, or rotating chairperson provisions to break impasses without winding up the venture.
  • Change of control: Trigger pre-emptive rights or forced buy-out if a Chinese unitholder undergoes a merger, state acquisition, or sanctions designation.
  • IP licensing: If the joint venture commercialises technology, the trust deed should cross-reference a separate IP licence agreement, clarifying that IP vests in the trustee as legal owner but is licensed exclusively to the operating entity.

Case Study: Mining Services JV via Unit Trust

Consider a mid-tier Australian mining services company partnering with a Chinese engineering conglomerate to bid for infrastructure contracts in Western Australia. The parties establish a unit trust where the Australian firm holds 60% of units and provides operational expertise, while the Chinese partner holds 40% and contributes proprietary equipment and access to Asian supply chains.

The trustee is an Australian company with three directors: two nominated by the Australian partner and one by the Chinese partner. The trust deed mandates that all distributions be made in proportion to unit holdings, qualifying the trust as a fixed trust. The Chinese investor routes its investment through a Hong Kong holding company to benefit from the Hong Kong–Australia DTA’s zero percent withholding on certain dividends, subject to the holding company meeting substance requirements under Hong Kong’s revised foreign-sourced income exemption regime.

Key outcomes:

  • The Australian partner retains majority governance and day-to-day control.
  • The Chinese partner receives predictable dividend streams and a clear exit path via a pre-agreed valuation formula.
  • The structure avoids Australian land tax surcharges because the unit trust does not hold residential or commercial real estate, only equipment and contract rights.
  • FIRB approval is obtained at formation, with ongoing compliance managed through annual reporting.

Tax Residency and Permanent Establishment Risks

A recurring challenge in any China Australia business structure is ensuring the trust remains Australian-resident for tax purposes. If the central management and control of the trust shifts to China—for example, because the Chinese appointor exercises substantive decision-making power from Beijing—the trust may become a foreign trust, losing access to Australia’s CGT discount and franking credit system.

To mitigate this risk:

  • Hold trustee board meetings physically in Australia, with detailed minutes.
  • Ensure the Australian resident directors make all strategic decisions, including distribution resolutions and major asset acquisitions.
  • Limit the Chinese party’s veto rights to protective provisions rather than affirmative management powers.
  • Consider a special purpose Australian holding company as trustee, with independent professional directors.

Chinese investors also face permanent establishment (PE) risks in Australia. A fixed place of business PE can arise if the Chinese parent sends employees to Australia to manage the joint venture’s operations. The ATO’s guidance on PE and dependent agents has tightened since 2024, so secondment arrangements should be structured as service agreements between the Chinese parent and the Australian trustee, with clear delineation of authority.

Succession and Exit Planning

Trust structures inherently require long-term thinking. For discretionary trusts, the perpetuity period (typically 80 years in most states) provides ample runway, but family succession among Chinese investors can introduce complexity. A discretionary trust investment that names individual Chinese family members as beneficiaries may require amendment when the next generation assumes control of the family business. Drafting the trust deed with a broad class of beneficiaries and a flexible amendment power—subject to trustee and appointor consent—can accommodate generational transitions without triggering a resettlement of the trust.

For unit trusts, exit mechanisms are paramount. Chinese state-owned investors often have defined investment horizons (5–10 years) and require a guaranteed exit. A common solution is a put option exercisable against the Australian partner at fair market value, with valuation determined by an independent expert using discounted cash flow or comparable company multiples. The trust deed should specify whether the put price is subject to any discount for lack of marketability or minority interest, as disputes on this point are frequent.

Emerging Trends in 2026

Several developments are reshaping the use of trusts in Australia-China joint ventures:

  • Increased ATO data matching: The ATO now receives bulk data from Chinese tax authorities under the Common Reporting Standard, enabling cross-verification of trust distributions declared to Chinese beneficiaries.
  • State tax reform: Victoria and Queensland are consulting on expanding foreign surcharges to unit trusts, which may erode the tax advantage of unit trusts over companies.
  • Digital asset joint ventures: Chinese blockchain firms are exploring Australian discretionary trust structures to hold digital assets, raising novel questions about the situs of crypto for state duty purposes.
  • Sanctions screening: Trustees must now screen Chinese counterparties against the Australian Sanctions Office consolidated list, with mandatory reporting of any suspected breaches.

Practical Steps for Establishing a Compliant Trust JV

  1. Engage specialist advisers early: Cross-border trust structuring requires coordinated input from Australian and Chinese legal, tax, and FIRB specialists. Do not rely on generic trust deeds.
  2. Model the tax outcomes: Prepare a multi-year financial model comparing a unit trust, discretionary trust, and Australian proprietary company, factoring in withholding taxes, DTA relief, state surcharges, and exit CGT.
  3. Document the commercial agreement: Before finalising the trust deed, negotiate a detailed heads of agreement covering governance, funding, IP, and exit. The trust deed should reflect, not create, the commercial bargain.
  4. Obtain FIRB approval upfront: Even if the investment falls below monetary thresholds, national security screening may apply. Voluntary notification provides certainty and avoids divestment orders.
  5. Implement robust compliance systems: Trustees should maintain a compliance calendar tracking distribution resolutions, ATO withholding obligations, FIRB conditions, and AUSTRAC reporting.
  6. Plan for dispute resolution: Agree on arbitration rules and seat at the outset. Including a mediation step before arbitration can preserve relationships in this culturally nuanced partnership model.

An Australian trust joint venture offers Chinese investors and Australian operators a uniquely adaptable platform for collaboration, blending tax efficiency with asset protection. Whether structured as a discretionary trust investment for family-backed ventures or a unit trust cross-border entity for institutional co-investment, success hinges on meticulous drafting, proactive tax compliance, and a clear-eyed assessment of regulatory obligations. As bilateral economic ties continue to deepen into 2026 and beyond, the trust remains a cornerstone of sophisticated China Australia business structure design.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can a Chinese company be the sole trustee of an Australian unit trust? A: Technically possible, but rarely advisable. An Australian-resident trustee is generally required to maintain the trust’s Australian tax residency and avoid adverse FIRB and state surcharge outcomes. Chinese companies can act as co-trustees or appointors with carefully limited powers.

Q: What is the stamp duty cost of transferring units in a cross-border unit trust? A: Stamp duty on unit transfers varies by state. In most jurisdictions, duty applies only if the trust holds land (directly or indirectly). For non-land trusts, unit transfers are typically duty-free. However, state revenue offices may aggregate related transfers to deem a landholder duty liability, so structuring advice is essential.

Q: How does the Australia-China DTA affect trust distributions? A: The DTA reduces withholding tax on dividends, interest, and royalties paid to Chinese residents. For trust distributions, the character flows through: a distribution sourced from dividends may qualify for the 15% rate, while interest income may be reduced to 10%. The Chinese recipient must meet beneficial ownership and limitation-of-benefits tests.

Q: Are discretionary trusts recognised under Chinese law for asset protection? A: China does not have a domestic trust law equivalent to Australia’s, but Chinese courts increasingly recognise the validity of offshore trust structures, particularly those established in Hong Kong or Singapore. For asset protection to be effective, the trust must be irrevocable and the settlor must not retain substantive control.
